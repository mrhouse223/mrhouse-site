Ship Small, Settle Fast

My biggest problem as a solo builder is not a lack of ideas.

It is that every idea immediately becomes ten products.

I can sit down to improve one page and somehow end up planning a launchpad, a screener, a holder scoring system, a liquidity layer, an agent platform, and an entirely new chain deployment.

In my head, it all connects.

In practice, trying to build the full vision at once is how nothing gets finished.

AI has made this problem even more dangerous. One person can now move unbelievably fast. I can turn an idea into working code in a night, even if I did not know exactly how to build it when I sat down.

That speed is powerful, but it can also create the illusion that everything should be built immediately.

The roadmap grows faster than the product.

I have had plenty of nights where I worked for hours, changed half the application, opened twenty new problems, and ended the session with something less deployable than when I started.

It feels productive because a lot happened.

Users do not care how much happened. They care whether something useful went live.

Mintd started getting better when I treated each feature as its own complete release.

The launchpad needed to work before it needed to become an ecosystem.

The screener could be useful before becoming a full replacement for every analytics platform.

The holder dashboard only needed to answer one important question: who actually held, and for how long?

The Furnace did not need a giant explanation. It needed to let someone burn a token or LP position in seconds.

Each release gave me something more useful than another week of planning. It gave me information.

People clicked it or they did not. They understood it or they got confused. Something broke. Someone requested a feature I had not considered. The real roadmap started replacing the imaginary one.

My current rule is simple: find the smallest version that is genuinely useful and put it in front of people.

Not a mockup. Not a thread promising what is coming. Something they can actually use.

Then watch.

Small does not mean careless, especially in crypto. If a feature touches user funds, contracts, permissions, or liquidity, moving quickly cannot mean skipping basic security.

Crypto punishes bad assumptions with real money. I have learned that lesson.

The goal is not to rush. The goal is to reduce the size of each decision so it can be properly finished, tested, and understood.

A good release should do three things:

  1. Give the user something they can use now.
  2. Teach me something about what should be built next.
  3. Leave the product in a more complete state than before.

That is enough.

I still have the instinct to disappear for a weekend and build the entire future of Mintd in one sitting. Sometimes that energy is exactly what gets a product across the line.

But intensity needs direction.

A hundred unfinished ideas are not infrastructure. One small tool that works is.

Ship the useful part. Make it real. Let people touch it. Fix what breaks. Then build the next block.

The larger vision will still be there tomorrow.
